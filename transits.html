Enter orbit data by hand

Paste the ISS two-line element set (the name line is optional). Leave this empty to fetch the current one from CelesTrak.

    The band is the strip of ground where the ISS crosses the Moon; the line down its middle is the centreline. The ring is how far you said you would travel.

    Reading the results

    The ISS is about 400 km up and the Moon is 384,000 km away, so the two only line up along a strip of ground a few kilometres wide, and the crossing is over in a second or two. Each result gives the time, the nearest point on the centreline, and how far that is from you.

    Light decides what the photo looks like. Around dusk and dawn the station is still in sunlight while your sky is dark, so it shows up lit, with detail. Later at night it is in Earth's shadow and crosses as a black silhouette, which only shows against the lit part of the Moon. In daytime the Moon is pale and contrast is low.

    How far to trust it

    The geometry is good to a few tens of metres. The limit is the orbit data: the station's published orbit drifts and is occasionally boosted, so a prediction a week out can move by kilometres and seconds. Run the search again the day before and once more a few hours ahead, and set up on the centreline, not the edge of the strip.

    Elevation is taken as yours along the whole path, and refraction near the horizon is not modelled, which is one reason to prefer a Moon well above 15°.
